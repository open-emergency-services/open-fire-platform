# How this project is run, funded, and what I promise

*The honest version. Plain language. No mission-washing.*

If you're going to consider trusting this software with your department's data, you
deserve to know exactly who's behind it, how it stays alive, and where the risks
are. Here it is, straight.

## Who runs this (today)

One person. This project is maintained by [your name] and operated through an
existing company, **[Your LLC]**. There is no board, no venture capital, no
outside investors, and — right now — no nonprofit foundation. If you were picturing
a big organization behind this, there isn't one yet. That's on purpose (see below).

## Why an LLC, and not a nonprofit (yet)

Standing up and running a nonprofit is real, continuous administrative work —
paperwork, a board, filings, compliance. I'm not going to spend that time before
the project has proven it can actually help departments and pay for itself. That
effort belongs in the code first.

I already have an LLC, and it's the lowest-overhead way to do two necessary things
legally: take hosting and sponsorship money, and pay contributors. If and when the
project grows enough that a foundation genuinely makes sense — grants that require
one, multiple maintainers, real community governance — I'll set one up and move the
code, trademark, and community stewardship into it, with the LLC continuing to run
paid hosting/support (the standard "nonprofit + company" split many open-source
projects use). The triggers for that are listed below. Until then, the honest
structure is: **a solo maintainer and an LLC.**

## I'd like to make a living from this — and that's not the thing you should worry about

Two things are true at once: I'm building this because the private-equity pricing
situation is genuinely bad for fire departments, and I'd also like this to
eventually pay me for my time. I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

A founder earning a fair living from **hosting and support** is not the same as the
"free until you're locked in, then squeeze" playbook you've seen. The difference is
entirely in the promises below. **Judge me on those promises, not on whether I make
money.** If I ever break one, you have every right to call it out and walk — and the
promises are specifically designed so that walking is always easy.

## The promises (what makes this different from a VC/PE play)

1. **The code stays open.** It's Apache-2.0 and it stays that way. No "source
   available later," no bait-and-switch relicense, no pulling the rug once there's
   an install base.
2. **Your data is always yours.** Open formats, one-click export, no exceptions.
   Leaving is always possible. There is no hostage — which is the whole reason this
   project exists.
3. **We charge for service, never for features.** Every capability lives in the free
   code. Paid hosting buys convenience, compliance, backups, and support — not
   access. There will never be a crippled "community edition" with the good parts
   locked behind a paywall.
4. **Pricing is public.** Hosting prices and what drives them are posted in the
   open. No "call for a quote," no per-seat surprises, no quiet annual increases.
5. **I won't sell you to a roll-up.** I will not sell this project or its customer
   relationships to a private-equity consolidator. Because the code is open and your
   data is portable, no future owner could trap you anyway — but I'm saying it
   plainly regardless.
6. **If I walk away, you're not stranded.** See the wind-down guarantee below.

## The honest risk (please read this before you depend on it)

This might stay a nights-and-weekends project. It might never grow to cover every
module. For a while, it might just be me. I'd rather tell you that than oversell.

Here's why that's still a safe bet for you: because the code is open and your data
exports cleanly, a one-maintainer project is a *survivable* dependency. If I get hit
by a bus or lose interest, you can self-host, fork it, or hire anyone to run it. You
are never trapped by my bandwidth.

What I'd ask of you in return: while this is early and unproven, **keep the free
self-host path (or your current system) as a fallback** rather than betting your
federal-compliance obligations solely on a solo project. Adopt it because it earns
your trust module by module — not on faith.

## Wind-down guarantee

If I ever stop maintaining this, I commit to: (a) posting a clear public notice
rather than going dark; (b) leaving the code, docs, and container images available;
(c) providing a documented export/migration path off any hosted service; and (d)
handing the repository and trademark to a willing successor or foundation if one
exists. Nobody gets stranded mid-incident-season.

## The phases (structure grows only when money justifies it)

**Phase 1 — Now.** Solo maintainer + the existing LLC. Free OSS and self-host.
Build the Wave-0 NERIS-reporting wedge and earn a few design-partner departments.
Near-zero admin. No revenue expected or required.

**Phase 2 — First revenue.** Turn on **managed hosting** (run by the LLC,
cost-plus, prices published) for departments that don't want to self-host, plus
**sponsorship** via Open Collective / GitHub Sponsors. Hosting margin and
sponsorship offset my time and start paying contributors for specific work. This is
the point where the admin is worth it, because it's making money.

**Phase 3 — If it grows.** When one or more triggers below are hit, stand up a
nonprofit foundation to hold the code, trademark, and community and to accept
grants, while the LLC continues to operate hosting/support. Only then do I take on
that overhead.

**Phase 3 triggers (any of):**
- Recurring hosting revenue can reliably support more than one person.
- A grant worth pursuing (e.g., FEMA/DHS/state fire-marshal) requires a nonprofit.
- More than a handful of active contributors need real, shared governance.
- Departments or states want a vendor-neutral entity to contract with at scale.

## How contributors get paid

Early on, contributor time is offset through sponsorship and any hosting margin —
as targeted bounties and small retainers for specific work, tracked transparently
(Open Collective makes the ledger public). I'm the sole maintainer by default. I
don't *want* it to stay that way — bringing others in, and paying them fairly, is
the point of building a revenue base at all.

## Accountability

Think I've broken a promise on this page? **Open an issue and say so in public.**
That's the accountability mechanism, and it's a feature, not a bug. This document is
versioned in git; its history is the record of what I said and when.
